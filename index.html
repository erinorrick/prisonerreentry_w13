From Problem to Brief

A structured outline for your policy brief, built on your own population.

Before You Start

This activity turns your Milestone 3 problem statement into a structured outline for your policy brief. You will work through five parts: the problem, your key evidence, a stress test of this week's framework, a policy option, and the strongest argument against it. At the end, the activity assembles your outline and checks it against the two-page limit.

Have these open:

  • Your Milestone 3 problem statement and background draft
  • Your Milestone 2 annotated bibliography
  • This week's readings, especially Kazemian (2021)

Everything in the outline comes from you. The activity organizes and checks your work; it does not supply evidence or arguments. Nothing you type here is saved if you close or reload the page, so plan to finish in one sitting or copy your work as you go.

Part 2: Key Evidence

List three or four evidence points your brief will rest on. For each one, classify the kind of claim it supports and who it was tested on. That second question is the differential lens (CLO 4): a finding tested on one group does not automatically apply to another.

Type of claimWhat it can showExample from the course
DescriptiveHow many, how often, what a group looks likeMaruschak et al. (2021): the profile of veterans in prison in 2016
AssociationTwo things go together; does not show that one causes the otherWong & Hickman (2022): a prior removal did not predict rearrest
CausalOne thing changes another; requires a design that rules out other explanationsAizer & Doyle (2015, Week 12): random assignment of judges
Program or policy evaluationOutcomes for people in a program or under a policy; whether it shows an effect depends on the designBaldwin & Hartley (2022): outcomes for veterans treatment court participants, with no comparison group

None of your evidence was tested on your population.

That is common for these populations, and it is worth saying in your brief. Explain the gap: what group the evidence comes from, why you think it does or does not transfer, and what evidence you would want instead. (At least 20 words.)

Part 3: Stress-Test the Framework

Kazemian (2021) makes three claims about desistance that run through this week. Your population may fit them, fit them partly, or push back against them. For each claim, decide and give a one- or two-sentence reason grounded in your evidence.

Keep in mind: Kazemian herself acknowledges that a small group remains active in crime later in life and says they should be assessed case by case. A claim that is true on average can still leave room for the public safety concerns about specific individuals.

Part 4: Policy Option

Propose at least one specific policy option. "More funding for reentry" is a direction, not an option. A specific option says what changes, who does it, and which of your evidence points supports it.

Part 5: Both Perspectives and the Strongest Opposition

Rehabilitative perspective: emphasizes desistance with age and time, the limited evidence behind many restrictions, and the role of care, benefits, and stability in successful reentry.

Public safety perspective: emphasizes the seriousness of some offenses, the specific risks the data do show, the stake of victims and communities, and the government's interest in monitoring or removing people it considers dangerous.

Your brief will make a recommendation, but it should not pretend the other perspective is absent. Start by stating how each perspective would view your Option 1.
